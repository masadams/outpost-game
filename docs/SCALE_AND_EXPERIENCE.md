# OUTPOST — Scale, Wonder, and the Player's Expanding Horizon
**Version:** 1.0 · **Date:** 2026-11-04 · **Status:** Experience specification and preserved design ideas  
**Companions:** [Master Design Bible](MASTER_DESIGN_BIBLE.md) · [Master Roadmap](MASTER_ROADMAP.md)

## 1. The emotional progression is a fundamental feature
OUTPOST should convey an extraordinary change in scale without losing the intimate feel of manual flight.

> Early game: **One asteroid is a successful mission.**  
> Mid-game: **One moon is an expedition.**  
> Late game: **One solar system is a productive home.**  
> Endgame: **Several star systems are a living civilization — but one small lander can still be worth flying.**

Growth should be visible and physical: from a little landing pad and a few wires, to refineries and defenses covering a curved horizon, to orbital docks, autonomous freight, remote industrial colonies and a map of systems crossed.

It is not simply “more ore” or “bigger numbers.” The player's **unit of concern**, **time horizon**, **spatial horizon**, and **personal agency** change.

## 2. A ladder of scale and meaningful firsts
These stages are experiential rather than rigidly gated chapters; a creative or skillful player may skip ahead.

| Stage | What feels enormous | Meaningful first achievement | Primary concern | New vantage |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 0. Stranded | A short flight above the landing site | Return alive with a single asteroid's ore | Fuel, landing, hull, oxygen | Your landing pad |
| 1. First outpost | A small mining/refining operation | Turn finite ore into a new facility | Power, storage, manufacturing | Planet's horizon |
| 2. Warning | The space around home | Detect, prepare for and survive first asteroid stream | Interception, damage, repairs | Observatory's sky map |
| 3. First moon | The distance between worlds | Survey/land/mine/return with uncommon material | Navigation and braking | Orbital chart |
| 4. Shipbuilder | The contrast between craft | Create a scout or heavy mining ship that changes tactics | Mass, thrust, efficiency | Shipyard |
| 5. Planetary industry | Two or more operational sites | Mine elsewhere and supply home | Resupply and logistics | System map |
| 6. Interstellar pioneer | The journey to another star | Send a probe, then personally visit another system | Distance, shielding and return | Stellar map |
| 7. Network builder | Multiple functioning star systems | Supply and maintain outposts across stars | Fleet, reliability and production | Galactic operations |
| 8. Frontier explorer | The still-unknown beyond a large network | Discover an unexplained world or anomalous signal | Judgment and curiosity | New horizons |

**Do not erase earlier scale.** Even after automating mining, a single hazardous asteroid might contain a unique material or pose a special piloting challenge. A late-game landing on a high-gravity world should still be tense.

## 3. Four dimensions of scale
### 3.1 Physical scale
At low altitude, the player sees rocks, the pad, turbines and the lander; at high altitude, the base dwindles to a point; farther out, the whole curved planet is visible; later, planets are points against a star; eventually the system and then the nearby stars become the navigable territory.

Camera modes may blend or change scale, but they represent locations in one world. Never imply a seam that teleports the player without an explicit chosen travel system. Labels, scale bars and map indicators should preserve orientation.

### 3.2 Time scale
An early sortie may last a few minutes. A planned lunar journey could span many burns and decisions. Remote industry operates while the player explores. Interstellar journeys demand route planning, potentially time acceleration or faster drives. Do not force pointless waiting merely to prove the universe is large.

**Open:** how time acceleration handles threats, simulation fidelity, remote production and manual control. A long-distance “coast” may be compressed in player time while still accumulating hazards, wear and resource use.

### 3.3 Economic scale
One asteroid initially matters because its materials can fund an actual build. Later, a mining operation handles many asteroids, but maintenance, haulage and energy create genuine costs. A rare asteroid can still be extremely valuable due to composition, not just quantity.

Players should experience increasing production *orders of magnitude* without a UI that becomes accounting-heavy. Present inventories, rates, shortages and priorities at the appropriate level of zoom; reveal detail on demand.

### 3.4 Responsibility scale
Start by keeping one ship alive. Expand to protecting a base, provisioning an expedition, maintaining a moon mine, coordinating defenses, running a supply route and deciding where to found the next colony. The player chooses when to delegate operational details.

## 4. Vistas, landmarks and anticipation
Use visible landmarks to motivate ambition before the player can reach them:
- A moon rises over the starter planet before it is mapped precisely.
- A distant dot on the telescope resolves into a star with possible planets.
- A debris belt or enormous crater is recognizable from orbit.
- A newly built orbital installation becomes visible from the home surface.
- Ships and stations that the player built become landmarks on subsequent trips.
- A far-off planet appears intriguing long before a ship can survive arrival.

The map should progress from **local radar** → **orbital map** → **planetary system survey** → **stellar chart**, with certainty and knowledge levels visible. More information is earned through instruments and probes.

## 5. Representative narrative of one save (not a scripted mandatory storyline)
**Opening:** Your small ship has limited cargo. You spot an asteroid, climb under thrust, break it into ore, catch enough fragments and nurse the ship back onto its pad. The cargo makes a solar array or refinery possible.

**Early base:** You construct power, processing and fuel. Ore is still precious; careless crashes and wasted propellant matter. A Research Lab opens a sensor improvement.

**First warning:** An Observatory detects a moving field of objects with an approximate arrival window. You build a turret and battery, perhaps sacrificing a new mining project to do so. The storm passes under real gravity: some rocks miss or hit elsewhere, one damages a facility, and another leaves a mineral sample worth studying.

**New reach:** You discover a moon and a mineral deposit. The ship can perhaps get there immediately, but carrying enough fuel to return or extracting the deposit demands a careful upgrade. You design a more efficient craft, land and return.

**Industrial foothold:** Your lunar rig begins producing material. What used to require a personal round trip now moves through a physical cargo route. Building an orbital fuel depot changes what journeys are feasible.

**Interstellar ambition:** A telescope identifies an interesting nearby star. An automated probe surveys it. You build a shielding-capable long-range vessel and cross a dangerous region of debris, eventually braking into the new system.

**Large network:** Multiple stars have active facilities, ships, power supplies, freight and maintenance. The galactic chart shows the civilization's footprint — while unexplored dark regions remain. You can still choose to fly a simple lander manually into an uncharted valley.

## 6. Principles that preserve scale
1. **No instant minimization of old accomplishments.** Automation should free the player, not make every early mission irrelevant.
2. **No arbitrary travel gates.** Space is vast because of physical and logistical conditions, not invisible game boundaries.
3. **No infinite resources for convenience.** Even high production needs material throughput.
4. **No fake urgency.** Events should have physical causes and meaningful warning.
5. **No universal autopilot too soon.** Manual skills should develop before automation replaces repetition.
6. **No automatic level-scaled danger.** A distant environment is harsh because it is harsh, not because of player rank.
7. **No forced filler transit.** Real distance should mean planning, expense, hazard and anticipation rather than watching nothing for hours.
8. **No early promise of millions of handcrafted worlds.** Diversity, depth and memorable places are more valuable than a high planet count.

## 7. Concrete presentation devices
- **Seamless zoom:** camera and map transitions convey increasing altitude and enormous distance; coordinates and velocity stay meaningful.
- **Relative-size cues:** ship against asteroid, ship against base, base against horizon, moon against local orbit, planets against solar system.
- **Construction silhouettes:** solar panels and a small hut first, arrays and factories next, eventually orbital rings of activity and freight traffic (not necessarily a literal ring structure).
- **Visible infrastructure:** lights on the surface, tracked cargo vessels, scanner sweeps and remote installation markers.
- **Rituals of first contact:** first detection, first orbit, first moon landing, first rescue, first outbound probe and first interstellar arrival receive understated, memorable feedback.
- **Preserved scars:** craters, wrecks, repaired stations, and exhausted mines remind the player how much has changed.
- **No false omniscience:** chart labels distinguish guessed position from confirmed orbit, and observed stock from estimated output.
- **Scale-sensitive UI:** instruments when piloting, facility panel when building, command/logistics view when operating systems. Do not overload the lander HUD with galaxy-wide data.

## 8. Design tensions that need actual answers
These are not reasons to stop writing the bible; they are future decisions best informed by playable experiments.

### Q1. How large is one system in game units?
Choose planetary and interplanetary distances so flight feels physical, moons are achievable, and travel is not dull. Current alpha uses intentionally compact physical scales. A later system should not blindly adopt real astronomical distances if they erase playability.

### Q2. How big is the interstellar universe?
A large fixed or growing seeded stellar region both work. The player's *perceived* scale comes from diverse, memorable environments and meaningful investment in traversing them, not a literal infinite coordinate count. Exact system count is open.

### Q3. How do we respect long travel without enforced idleness?
Investigate acceleration/coasting, safe time warp, remote operations and advanced continuous drives. Warp rules must not bypass hazards, resource expenditure or consequences.

### Q4. Does the player inhabit one pilot?
A single mortal pilot makes danger intimate but may obstruct late-game fleets and rescue. Switchable ships enable a civilization-scale fantasy. A hybrid (pilot local missions, command through outposts/remote terminals) is a strong candidate.

### Q5. Does combat include enemies?
Natural hazards and occasional defensive threats are already strong. Hostile machines, aliens or factions remain exploratory; their addition should serve the scale progression, not transform the game into endless combat.

### Q6. How much variation between starting seeds?
A familiar standardized home world helps onboarding; balanced variant worlds improve replayability. Ensure every start can bootstrap fuel, research and escape without unwinnable RNG.

### Q7. What does the world remember?
Determine the persistence granularity for terrain deformation, impacts, wrecks, ore depletion, derelicts and remote activity. Preserve high-meaning scars and decisions; aggregate trivial clutter.

### Q8. What does an advanced ship make possible?
Advanced drives should shorten interstellar journeys while rockets, RCS and specialized landing craft remain necessary. Avoid a super-engine that trivializes every challenge.

### Q9. Can discoveries change the research order?
Prefer yes: study the phenomenon you actually encountered, sometimes out of order. Basic survival must not rely on one rare randomized event.

### Q10. What exactly does “continuous” mean on the technical side?
It means no arbitrary gameplay barriers or fake separated mission levels. Implementation may stream zones, change precision and use analytic orbital state; it must preserve trajectory, inventory, position and causal continuity.

## 9. Scale acceptance checklist for every major milestone
- [ ] Does the feature create a new capability, challenge, perspective or meaningful choice?
- [ ] Does the player still understand the relationship between local and large-scale movement?
- [ ] Does an earlier hard-won accomplishment retain narrative or practical significance?
- [ ] Are new travel capabilities earned through physics, engineering, discovery or infrastructure rather than invisible locks?
- [ ] Are resources and time accounted for without deadening the pace?
- [ ] Does this support both hands-on piloting and eventual larger operational responsibility?
- [ ] Can a player see or infer what to aspire to next without a forced quest marker?
- [ ] Will this remain understandable with two planets, twenty planets and multiple stars?

## 10. Ideas preserved for future expansion
These are **not** mandatory features; preserve them as ideas so they are not forgotten:
- Gravitational assists and orbital transfer optimization.
- Salvage and recovery of permanently drifting ships.
- Modular ships: scouts, interceptors, miners, transports and carriers with deployable small landers.
- Shielding for debris and radiation; atmospheric landing differences.
- Refinery specialization, rare deposits, unique research samples and high-energy mining tools.
- Track and deflect asteroids, not merely shoot them; mineral-rich captured objects.
- Orbital fuel depots, repair platforms, construction yards and mass drivers.
- Surface cargo rovers, mining drones, automated haulage and colony packages.
- Navigation beacons, probe networks and remote telescope data.
- Binary-star systems, rogue planets, dark regions and young debris-rich stars.
- Ancient structures, unexplained signals, derelict spacecraft and optional overarching mysteries.
- Comets, recurring orbital debris streams, solar events and system-scale opportunities.
- Terrain excavation, craters, persistent wrecks and player-created landmarks.
- Engineering an entire planet or industrial region over very long play.
- Research unlocking ways to survive or work in environments that are physically reachable from the start.

Detailed tasks and their acceptance criteria belong in the roadmap; systems, philosophy and open ideas belong in the bible; actual shipped behavior belongs in current feature docs.


## 12. Distinctiveness across every scale
See [Distinctive Experience & Design Differentiation](DISTINCTIVE_EXPERIENCE.md). The sense of scale is not an excuse to build increasingly large feature lists: each new horizon should emerge from interactions already meaningful at small scale. A rock worth recovering in the opening can become an orbital navigation hazard, a captured mine or a historical landmark later. The first mining sortie, physical storm and first interplanetary expedition deserve exceptional polish before breadth. Players should describe **what they made happen** rather than only the size of the map.
